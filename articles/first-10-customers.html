How to get your first 10 customers without a big ad budget

Tell people who already know you

You can look for your first customers without a large advertising budget. You need a plain description of the work, a short list of people to tell, and a simple way for someone to say yes. These steps start the conversations. They are not a forecast.

Start with former workmates, neighbours, old customers and businesses you already buy from. Send one personal note. Say what you do, who it is for, where you work and how to book. A short paragraph is easier to forward than a brochure.

Make it true before you send it. For example: "I have started a mobile bookkeeping service for trade businesses in Geelong. I look after the weekly books and the BAS. If you know a sparky or a plumber who wants that taken off their plate, they can text me." Change it so every detail is true for you.

That note is not a mailing list. Commercial email and texts sit under the Spam Act 2003, which expects consent. If you are unsure, ask the person or keep the message one-to-one. Before you email a list, read the spam guidance from the Australian Communications and Media Authority, or ask a professional.

Ask for a specific introduction

"Tell your friends" is vague. "A cafe owner near Parramatta who wants a regular cleaner" is something a person can picture. Give them one sentence to forward, plus your phone number or booking link.

Thank them whether or not the introduction turns into work. If you offer a thank-you, tell both people what it is. Do not hide a commission, and do not promise a discount you have not costed. After you have finished a job, you can ask that customer once for an introduction.

Post in local groups only where the rules allow

Read the rules of a Facebook group or community noticeboard first. Many groups keep promotion to a weekly thread. Use that thread, post once, and do not disguise an advertisement as a question.

State the service, the area, what is included and how to get in touch. Name an insurance policy only if you hold it. Answer questions directly.

A chamber note, a pinboard or a buy-swap-sell group has rules too. The Australian Business Startup Facebook group limits promotion. Read those rules before you post. Paid placements there are labelled. This page is not a paid placement.

Fill in a Google Business Profile

If people visit you, or you visit them, create a Google Business Profile. Google does not charge for the listing. It can show your name, category, hours, phone, website and service area on Search and Maps.

Use your trading name and a real category. If you travel to the customer, set a service area rather than a shop address you do not use. Add photos of your own work, and describe the job in ordinary words.

No reviews yet is a normal starting point. Do not review yourself, and do not ask a friend to pose as a customer. When a real customer is pleased, ask once and send the link. Do not offer a reward for a high rating. Google's help for that profile covers how to create it and how verification works.

Pair up with a business that does not compete

Look for someone who meets your customer at another moment: a painter and a cleaner, a groomer and a pet shop, a bookkeeper and a coach, a cafe and its baker. Agree one small action and write it down, such as swapping cards or mentioning each other when asked. Say how long you will try it.

Recommend only work you would trust with your own customer. If money is involved, say so. Either the introduction is unpaid, or there is a referral fee and the price the customer pays does not change.

Give them a small, priced first step

Offer one room, one hour, a first groom, or your smallest real package. Write the price, what it includes and how to book. If you are registered for GST, show consumers the GST-inclusive total. If you are not registered, do not add GST. The Australian Taxation Office publishes who must register. Pick a price you can deliver in a quiet week. Do not invent a ranking or a tally of customers.

Follow up on the day you named

If someone says not yet, record their name, the job and the day you said you would be in touch. Contact them that once. Stop if they decline. Keep a later date if they set one.

After the job, confirm what you did and how to reach you if a fix is needed. Ask once for a review on the profile you use, with the link in the same note. Jot down where the enquiry came from. That record is not a prediction.

Fictional example

Example business (fictional): a mobile dog groomer in Townsville

This business is made up. Sam is not a real person, and this box reports no results.

Sam grooms dogs at the owner's home in Townsville and the northern beaches. Sam messages people already saved in their phone, names the work days, what a basic groom includes and the price, and includes a sentence they can forward.

Sam posts once in the suburb group's weekly services thread, after reading the rules. Sam opens a Google Business Profile for a service area, with photos of Sam's own set-up, and adds no reviews.

For one month, Sam and a pet shop agree in writing that the shop may hand out Sam's card and Sam may name the shop if an owner asks where to buy food. Nobody pays a fee. The card offers a paid bath and brush on Tuesdays. A "maybe next month" gets one follow-up. A finished groom gets one review request. There is no claim here about how many jobs would follow.
