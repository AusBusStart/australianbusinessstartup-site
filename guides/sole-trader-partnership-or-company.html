Business structures

Sole trader, partnership or company

These are the three structures people compare first. None of them is the right answer for every business. This is a plain comparison of what each one is, what it costs to register, and where the risk sits. A trust is mentioned at the end because it is a real option, and it is a different conversation.

1What you are actually choosing

The structure decides who owns the business, who pays the tax, and who is responsible if it cannot pay its debts. It is not a logo and it is not a business name. business.gov.au's structures pages and the ATO's tax obligations by structure are the references. Use them, and a registered tax agent or a lawyer, before you choose for your own situation. This page is not that advice.

There are other structures: trust, co-operative, joint venture, Indigenous corporation. They are on the business.gov.au list. A trust in particular has its own deed, its own tax rules and often higher advice costs. If someone has told you to "just set up a trust", get that checked before you do it.

2Sole trader

business.gov.au describes a sole trader as the simplest structure, and relatively easy and inexpensive to set up. You are the business. You control the decisions, you report the business income in your individual tax return using your own tax file number, and you are personally responsible for the debts and losses.

The ATO makes a few points that surprise people:

  • You can employ staff. You cannot employ yourself.
  • You pay super for employees. Super for yourself is optional, though the ATO notes it is a sensible way to save for retirement.
  • You are entitled to an ABN, and you use that ABN for the business. Applying is free. See how to apply for an ABN.
  • You register for GST if your GST turnover is $75,000 or more, or if you provide taxi, limousine or ride-sourcing services, or if you want to claim fuel tax credits. More in the GST guide.
  • You keep financial records for at least five years.
  • A separate business bank account is recommended, and it is not required.
  • You cannot split business profits with family members.

Unlimited liability means your personal assets can be used to pay business debts. That is the trade-off for the simpler set-up.

3Partnership

A partnership is two or more people carrying on business together and sharing the income or the losses. business.gov.au says it is relatively easy and inexpensive to set up, with fewer reporting requirements than a company.

A partnership needs its own ABN and its own tax file number. It lodges a partnership tax return every year. The partnership itself does not pay income tax. Each partner reports their share in their own return and pays the tax on it. Partners are not employees of the partnership. They are responsible for their own super. The partnership pays super for its employees.

The ATO says a written partnership agreement is not required for a partnership to exist, and that if there is no agreement, income and losses are shared equally. An agreement is still worth having. business.gov.au suggests a lawyer reviews it. It should cover contributions, how profits and losses are split, who decides what, what happens if someone leaves or dies, and how you will end it.

In a general partnership, which is the common kind, every partner is responsible for the partnership's debts. A limited partnership is different: at least one general partner manages the business and has unlimited liability, and limited partners are passive investors whose liability is capped at what they contributed. Limited partnerships are registered with the state or territory, not set up by an ABN alone. Each state and territory has its own partnership Act. The business.gov.au partnership page lists them.

GST registration uses the same $75,000 turnover threshold as other businesses.

4Company

A company is a separate legal entity. business.gov.au says it can incur debt, sue and be sued. The money the business earns belongs to the company. Members are not liable for the company's debts beyond any amount unpaid on their shares. Directors can still be personally liable if they breach their legal duties.

business.gov.au also describes a company as more complex to start and run, with higher set-up and running costs, and as something that generally suits people who expect variable income and want to use losses against later profits. That is a description, not a recommendation.

A company has its own ABN and tax file number, lodges a company tax return, and is run by directors for the shareholders. Directors need a director identification number. The Australian Business Registry Services says you apply for your own director ID. Apply before you are appointed. If you are already a director and do not have one, apply now. It is free, you only apply once, and nobody can apply for you.

Companies are registered with ASIC under the Corporations Act 2001. Common ongoing duties include telling ASIC within 28 days of key changes, keeping financial records, an annual review, an annual review fee, and a solvency declaration from the directors.

business.gov.au's 1 July 2026 update said ASIC would increase company fees from that date:

  • registering a company rose from $611 to $636
  • the annual review fee for a proprietary company rose from $329 to $342

ASIC indexes fees, and the live figures sit on ASIC's fee indexation page. Confirm the number there before you pay. The amounts above are what business.gov.au published for the 1 July 2026 increase, checked for this page in October 2026. They are not a quote for every company type. A public company and a registered scheme pay a different annual review fee.

A company registers for GST at the same $75,000 turnover threshold ($150,000 for a non-profit organisation).

business.gov.au has a separate page on the difference between a sole trader and a company, including the tax differences.

6Changing later

Plenty of businesses start as a sole trader and register a company once the risk or the income justifies the extra admin. business.gov.au says a change of structure can mean you must cancel the existing ABN and apply for a new one. Do the tax and legal tidy-up at the same time: new registrations, new bank account for a company, and contracts moved into the right name.

The cheapest structure on day one is not always the cheapest structure if something goes wrong. That is the question to put to an accountant who can see your numbers, not to a form.
